A Letter You Have to Look At: Niki de Saint Phalle and Jean Tinguely Write to John Ashbery

By Adam Aslan

Most letters get read. This one has to be looked at. One creased sheet of paper is packed edge to edge with ink stamps, marker scrawls, stick figures and wheeled machines, with French handwriting in green, orange and red running through all of it. Two of the most inventive artists of 1960s Paris made it together for a friend, the poet and critic John Ashbery.

What the museum says

The sheet is in the collection of the Morgan Library & Museum in New York, which received about twenty-five works on paper from Ashbery's collection in 2019, a gift from his husband, David K. Kermani. It was shown in the Morgan exhibition Friends Who Came to See Me: Drawings from John Ashbery's Collection, which ran from May 1 to October 25, 2026. The wall label in my photograph matches the Morgan's published label text for the show. The Morgan's online record for this sheet, accession number 2019.171, lists it under Tinguely as "Untitled, (illustrated letter to John A.)," 1960s, stamps, pen and ink, and markers on paper, about 11 7/8 by 15 5/8 inches. The exhibition label and catalogue date it to about 1963.

A second Ashbery letter from the two artists, dated 1966, is a separate sheet with collaged paper and pink yarn (accession number 2019.170). It was on the same label, but it is not the one in the photograph.

Who they were

Saint Phalle (born 1930 in Neuilly-sur-Seine, France, died 2002 in La Jolla, California) and Tinguely (born 1925 in Fribourg, Switzerland, died 1991 in Bern) were longtime lovers and frequent collaborators, and later husband and wife. In Paris they were prominent figures in Nouveau Réalisme. According to the Morgan's catalogue, Ashbery took notice of Saint Phalle when he was reviewing art in Paris and she was showing her shooting paintings.

Dating the sheet

The catalogue dates this letter from a customized rubber stamp that reads "Auberge du Cheval Blanc, Soisy-sur-École," the former inn outside Paris that Saint Phalle bought with Tinguely and moved into in the winter of 1963 to 1964. That points to late 1963 or shortly after. A handwritten note also mentions the bar at the Maison de la Radio in Paris, which the catalogue reads as a plan to meet Ashbery on an unspecified Friday. The catalogue says both artists signed with dedications: she added "pour mon grand amour," and he wrote "amitiés & GRRRR."

Three hands on one page

The catalogue reads the sheet as a collaboration between Saint Phalle, Tinguely and a young child, most likely Saint Phalle's son Philip Matthews. It credits the child with the stick figures, the decals, the impressions from animal-shaped rubber stamps and misspelled nonsense phrases in marker, such as "maman pipi trouve plu le po," or "Mama pee-pee can't find the potty." Tinguely's contribution is the machine in black pen at lower left, wheels tied together with running belts. The catalogue notes that he drew machines like this in both letters and was at the time planning his first major public work, Eureka.

What I see

This part is my own reading of the photograph, not the museum's. A numbered ladder of circles from one to ten runs down the left side. Near it, what looks like a hippopotamus drinks beside a woman with a parasol, and a monkey sits on an arrow above. The name "John" appears twice, once in outlined blue letters beside a bold red arrow, and once stamped in blue. At the lower right is a box of handwriting that reads like directions. A camel stands in a speech bubble holding a single question mark, and a row of small red figures at the bottom edge each carry one too. The page reads as a note and as an invitation to play, both addressed to a friend who loved art.
